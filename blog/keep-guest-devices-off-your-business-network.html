Keep Guest Devices Off Your Business Network

Offering Wi-Fi to the people who walk through your door is good hospitality. The problem is not the person — it is the device in their pocket, and what it can reach once it is on your network.

Offering Wi-Fi to the people who walk through your door is good hospitality. A guest at your lodge, a supplier waiting at your farm office, a parent in the school reception, a customer in your shop — handing them the Wi-Fi password feels like the neighbourly thing to do. It usually is.

The problem is not the person. It is the device in their pocket.

You cannot see whether their phone has its security updates. You cannot see whether their laptop is already carrying something nasty from the last network it joined. You do not know who else has used it. And once that device is on the same network as your business computers, your point-of-sale, your cameras, and your file storage, it can reach further than it should.

The fix is simple, and most businesses already have the equipment for it. Put guests on their own separate network — one that gives them the internet and nothing else.

What a proper guest network actually does

When a visitor connects to a well-set-up guest network, they can do everything they came to do. Browse, check email, send a message, join a video call.

What they cannot do is reach into your business.

That means their device stays away from your computers and servers, your network printers, your file storage and backups, your security cameras, your gate and building access systems, and the admin pages of your own router. It also means it stays away from the other visitors on the guest network.

Both networks can share the same internet line. The separation does not happen in the sky — it happens inside your router, firewall, and wireless access points, in the settings. The Australian Cyber Security Centre puts it plainly: keep your internal network separate from the guest Wi-Fi used by devices you do not manage.

Why one shared network is a risk you do not need

Let us be fair about this. Putting a visitor on your main network does not hand them every file you own. Your passwords, your device settings, and your user permissions still stand in the way.

But a shared network lets a device find things it has no reason to touch — a printer, a shared folder, a camera feed, a storage drive, a login page that was never properly locked down. And if that visitor’s device is already infected, it will often go looking for other devices to spread to. On a flat, shared network, your business systems are right there in reach.

Split the network into separate sections and those unwanted connections get blocked before they arrive. The UK’s National Cyber Security Centre describes this well: dividing a network limits how far a single compromised device can move. One sick device should not be able to infect the whole building.

You are not adding security by locking every door inside the house. You are simply not inviting strangers past the front hall.

A second Wi-Fi name is not proof of anything

Here is the trap most small businesses fall into.

Your phone shows two Wi-Fi names — say, “Lodge-Office” and “Lodge-Guest.” Two names, two passwords, job done. Except the names (the technical term is SSID) tell you nothing about what sits behind them. A second name can still dump guests straight onto your business network.

What matters is the configuration. Proper business equipment puts guest traffic on its own separate lane — often called a VLAN — and then uses firewall rules to stop that lane from reaching your private systems. Many small-business routers do this automatically the moment you switch on “guest mode.” Cisco’s own guidance treats guest Wi-Fi exactly this way: a separate network, limited to the internet, walled off by firewall rules.

When you open your router settings, the features you are looking for go by names like:

  • Guest network
  • Network isolation, or “deny local network access”
  • Client isolation
  • VLAN
  • Guest firewall rules

If all you can find is a second Wi-Fi name and password, that is your sign to ask. Your IT provider can confirm — in plain terms — exactly what a guest device can and cannot reach.

Should guests be able to see each other?

Usually, no.

Without the right setting, one guest’s laptop can quietly contact another guest’s device on the same Wi-Fi. The setting that stops this goes by a few names — client isolation, wireless isolation, or peer-to-peer blocking. Turn it on.

It is worth being clear that this is a different control from keeping guests off your business network. One blocks guests from talking to each other. The other blocks guests from talking to your systems. A good guest network uses both.

Where should staff phones and personal laptops go?

A member of staff checking personal messages on their own phone only needs the internet. The guest network is the safer home for that device, and for any personal laptop that does not need to open company files.

The moment a personal device needs to reach your business systems, the rules change. If your team uses their own phones or laptops for company work, you need a clear bring-your-own-device policy. Those devices may need security software, encryption, and specific access rules before they are let in.

The principle is easy to hold in your head: company-owned, managed devices can use the business network when the work requires it. Personal and unmanaged devices stay on the restricted network unless there is a real business reason to give them more.

What about the smart TV, the cameras, and the rest?

Lodges have smart TVs. Shops have digital displays. Most premises now have cameras, speakers, or a thermostat that quietly talks to the internet.

These gadgets usually need an internet connection, but they almost never need to reach your computers. So they should not be sitting on the same network as your accounts and your customer records. Give them their own space. Some smaller businesses use the guest network for this, though that will not work if the device has to talk to a controller, an app, or a local recording box.

A security camera should not be able to reach the office PC simply because they share a router. CISA’s guidance says the same thing: use separate networks for guests, for connected gadgets, for personal devices, and for work devices.

Five quick checks for your guest Wi-Fi

You do not need to be technical to run through these — or to ask your IT provider to.

  1. Check who is on each network. Business computers and managed equipment belong on the business network. Visitors and unmanaged devices belong on the guest network.
  2. Confirm the guest network is walled off. A guest device should not be able to reach your printers, file storage, cameras, servers, or router admin pages.
  3. Check that guests are isolated from each other. Switch on client isolation if your equipment supports it.
  4. Review the Wi-Fi security. The Australian Cyber Security Centre recommends WPA3, or WPA2 where WPA3 is not available. Give the guest network a password that is different from your business one.
  5. Look after the equipment itself. Keep firmware current, turn on automatic updates where you can, replace the default admin password, and retire any router or access point that no longer gets security updates.

Run these checks again every time your router, firewall, switches, or access points are replaced or reconfigured. Settings have a habit of resetting when hardware changes.

Two questions worth settling now

Do I need a second internet line for guests?

Almost certainly not. One business router or firewall can run a single internet connection and keep the two networks apart. You can even cap the guest network’s speed so visitors do not slow down your own work. The exception is very old or very basic routers, which sometimes offer a guest option with little real control. If yours is one of those, check the manual or ask for it to be tested.

Should guests and staff share a Wi-Fi password?

No. Share your business password with visitors and you lose track of who can reconnect later. Worse, if you ever need to change it, you have to change it on every company device too. A separate guest password can be swapped out on its own — after a busy event, after a contractor finishes, or whenever too many people know it. Just remember: the password controls who gets onto the guest network. It does not separate the networks. That is a different job, done in the settings.

Frequently asked questions

Is a guest Wi-Fi password enough to protect my business?

No. The password decides who can connect. Your equipment still has to block guest devices from reaching your internal systems.

Can guests and staff use the same internet connection?

Yes. They can share one line while staying fully separated inside the router, firewall, and wireless equipment.

Can a visitor use our network printer?

Normally this should be blocked. If guest printing is genuinely needed, it can be set up in a controlled way without opening up the rest of your business.

Does a guest network stop cyberattacks?

No — and nothing does on its own. A guest network reduces unnecessary access and limits how far a compromised device can travel. You still need strong passwords, multi-factor authentication, updates, endpoint protection, and backups working alongside it.

The honest bottom line

Separating your guest Wi-Fi is one of the cheapest, highest-value things a small business can do. It does not stop every threat. It does quietly remove a whole category of them — and it means a stranger’s phone can never become a doorway into your business.

If you are not sure how your guest Wi-Fi is set up, do not guess. Ask your IT provider to confirm, in plain language, that it is properly separated from your business network.
