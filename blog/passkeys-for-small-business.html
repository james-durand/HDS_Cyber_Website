Passkeys: a stronger, simpler way to sign in

Most break-ins to a business account involve a stolen password, not clever hacking. Passkeys replace the password with something a scammer cannot phish, guess, or steal in a breach.

Most break-ins to a business account do not involve clever hacking. They involve a stolen password. Someone is tricked into typing it into a fake login page, reuses it across sites, or has it taken in a data breach somewhere else. The password is the weak link — and it has been for years.

Passkeys are the fix the whole industry has settled on. They replace the password with something a scammer cannot phish, guess, or steal in a breach. And for the person signing in, they are usually easier, not harder — a fingerprint, a face scan, or the same PIN you already use to unlock your phone.

This is not a fringe idea. The UK’s National Cyber Security Centre now treats passkeys as the default way to sign in and says passwords are no longer fit for purpose. Microsoft, Google, and Apple have built passkeys into the phones, laptops, and browsers your team already uses. Here is what a passkey actually is, why it is so much safer, and how your business can start using them — in plain terms.

What a passkey is

A passkey is a secure way to sign in to an account without typing a password.

When you create one, your device makes a pair of matching keys. One key — the private one — stays locked on your phone or laptop and never leaves it. The other — the public one — is handed to the website or service, which stores it. The public key is useless on its own; it is a lock, not a key.

To sign in, the service sends your device a one-time challenge. Your device answers it using the private key, but only after you approve the action with your fingerprint, your face, or your device PIN. The service checks the answer against the public key it holds, and lets you in.

A password is a secret you can be tricked into giving away. A passkey is a secret that never leaves your device — so there’s nothing to hand over.

The important part is what is not happening. No shared secret travels across the internet. Nothing gets typed into a login box. The website never learns anything that would let someone else sign in as you. Your fingerprint or face never leaves your device either — it only unlocks the key that is already there.

Why phishing can’t beat a passkey

This is where passkeys earn their keep.

A password can be handed over by mistake. A convincing fake login page — the kind that arrives in a “your mailbox is full” or “confirm your banking details” email — only has to fool a tired person for a second. Even the codes from an authenticator app can be typed into that fake page and used by the attacker within seconds.

A passkey closes that door. Each passkey is tied to the exact website it was made for. If a staff member lands on a lookalike page, the passkey simply will not work there — the web address does not match, so the device refuses to answer the challenge. There is no code to read out, no secret to paste, nothing for the attacker to catch in the middle. The FIDO Alliance, the body behind the standard, designed them this way on purpose: passkeys are phishing-resistant by nature, not by the user being careful.

For a small business, that matters enormously. It means an employee cannot accidentally give away the keys to your email, your accounting system, or your bank portal — even if they click the wrong link on a bad day.

Passkeys, passwords, and codes — the honest comparison

It helps to see where passkeys sit against what you use now.

A password is a secret you know and type. It can be guessed, reused, leaked in someone else’s breach, or phished. It is the thing attackers want most.

A password plus a code (ordinary multi-factor authentication) is much better — we recommend it on every account today — but it is not bulletproof. The code can still be phished, and some attackers wear people down with repeated approval prompts until someone taps “yes.”

A passkey rolls the “something you have” (your device) and “something you are” (your fingerprint or face) into one step. There is no code to intercept and no password to steal. It is both simpler for the user and stronger than the two combined.

None of this means your current MFA was a waste. It is doing real work right now, and it stays important until passkeys are in place. Passkeys are the next step up, not a sign you did the wrong thing before.

Two kinds of passkey

You will come across two flavours, and the difference is worth understanding.

Synced passkeys are backed up to your account — your Apple, Google, or Microsoft account — and appear on all your devices automatically. Make one on your phone and it is there on your laptop too. These are the convenient, everyday option for most businesses, and they survive a lost phone because they are safely backed up.

Device-bound passkeys live on one piece of hardware and never leave it — often a physical security key you plug in or tap. These give the highest level of protection and are worth it for your most sensitive logins: the administrator account for your email system, your banking, your core business platform. The trade-off is that you must keep the hardware safe and have a backup key.

Most small businesses use synced passkeys for the team and device-bound security keys for a handful of critical administrator accounts. You do not have to pick just one.

Where you can use passkeys already

You may be closer to this than you think. Passkeys already work with Microsoft 365 and Google Workspace — the two systems most lowveld businesses run their email on — as well as many banks, accounting packages, cloud storage services, and social platforms. The phones and laptops your team bought in the last few years almost certainly support them out of the box.

The usual pattern is simple: you keep your existing account, add a passkey to it, and from then on sign in with your fingerprint or face instead of a password. You can roll it out one service and one person at a time — there is no need to change everything at once.

How to start in a small business

You do not need a big project for this. A sensible order works best.

Begin with the accounts that would hurt most if they were stolen — your email administrator account first, then anyone who handles payments or sensitive information. These are the accounts attackers target, so they get the strongest protection first, ideally with a device-bound security key plus a backup.

Next, add passkeys for the rest of the team on their main business accounts, starting with email. Let people use the fingerprint or face unlock they already know. Keep your existing MFA switched on across everything while the rollout is in progress — you want the new protection in place before you lean on it.

Set up recovery before you need it. Every person should have at least two ways to get back in — a second passkey, a backup security key, or an approved recovery method your administrator controls — so a lost phone is an inconvenience, not a lockout. And as more of your team moves to passkeys, you can tighten the settings: your IT provider can begin steering people away from passwords on the services that support it.

Finally, keep a short record of which accounts have passkeys, on which devices, and who holds the backup keys. It is the same tidy-list habit that makes everything else in your business easier to secure.

What if someone loses their phone?

This is the first question everyone asks, and the answer is reassuring.

With synced passkeys, the passkey is backed up to the person’s Apple, Google, or Microsoft account — so when they set up a new phone and sign in, their passkeys come back with them. Losing the handset does not lose the passkey, and whoever finds the phone cannot use the passkey anyway, because it is locked behind the fingerprint, face, or PIN they do not have.

For device-bound keys, the rule is simple: always have a second one, kept somewhere safe. If the first is lost, the backup gets you in, and your administrator removes the missing key from the account.

Either way, the plan is the same one good businesses already use for keys to the building — have a spare, and know who holds it.

Frequently asked questions

Do passkeys replace passwords completely?

Eventually, for most logins, yes. For now you will often keep a password on the account as a fallback while passkeys roll out. The goal is to use the passkey every day and lean on the password less and less.

Is a fingerprint or face scan sent to the website?

No. Your fingerprint or face only unlocks the key stored on your own device. It never leaves the device and is never shared with the website or with us.

Are passkeys only for new or expensive devices?

No. Most phones and laptops from the last few years support them, as do the main web browsers. There is usually nothing to buy unless you choose physical security keys for your most sensitive accounts.

Can two people share one passkey for a shared mailbox?

Better not to. Each person should have their own passkey on their own device, even for a shared “accounts@” or “bookings@” mailbox. That way access can be given or removed one person at a time.

Do we still need MFA if we have passkeys?

A passkey already combines your device and your fingerprint or face, so it is strong multi-factor sign-in on its own. Keep your existing MFA running on any account that does not have a passkey yet.

The honest bottom line

Passwords have done their job for a long time, but they put the hardest part of security on the person least able to carry it — the busy employee trying to get through their day. Passkeys move that weight onto the device instead. They are harder to steal, impossible to phish in the usual way, and genuinely easier to use.

You do not have to switch everything overnight. Start with the accounts that matter most, keep your current MFA running alongside, and move the rest of the team across at a comfortable pace.
