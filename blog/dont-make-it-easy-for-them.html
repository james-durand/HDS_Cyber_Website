Don’t Make It Easy for Them

This October, the global cybersecurity community is speaking with one voice. Here is what that message means for a lodge in the Lowveld — and why ignoring it is no longer an option.

Every October, the world pauses to think about cybersecurity. Not because it’s a calendar tradition, but because the threats keep growing and the people most likely to pay the price — small businesses, families, community organisations — are still the least prepared.

This year, the US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) is running Cybersecurity Awareness Month under the banner “Securing the Next 250”, with a simple message behind it: don’t make it easy for them. No jargon. No acronyms. Just a direct challenge to every one of us who has ever reused a password, skipped a software update, or clicked a link without thinking twice.

In Hoedspruit and the broader Lowveld, that challenge is not abstract. We run lodges and guesthouses. We manage farms with networked sensors and smart gates. We process card payments, collect guest IDs, store booking data. We are connected — and that means we are exposed.

Your guests trust you with everything — including their cybersecurity.

That line is not marketing. It is a description of reality. When a guest checks in and connects to your Wi-Fi, books through your system, or hands over a credit card, they are trusting that you have done the basic things to keep their information safe. Most operators in this region have not. Not because they are careless — but because until recently, no one local was telling them where to start.

Why “Don’t Make It Easy” matters here

Cybercriminals are rational. They go where the defences are weak and the returns are reasonable. For years, that meant targeting large corporations with sophisticated attacks. But as big organisations hardened their perimeters, attackers shifted strategy. They moved downstream — to the suppliers, the subcontractors, the small businesses that feed into larger systems.

Tourism is exactly that kind of target. A mid-sized lodge in Limpopo holds hundreds of guest records, processes card transactions, and communicates with international booking platforms. The data is valuable. The defences are often minimal. And the reputational damage from a breach — in an industry built entirely on trust — is severe.

The Core 4: what “not making it easy” actually looks like

CISA’s campaign centres on a “Core 4”: four habits that eliminate the most common attack vectors. None of them are complicated. All of them require consistency.

  1. Recognise and report phishing. The fake SARS notice. The spoofed booking platform email. The WhatsApp message from a “supplier” asking you to update your banking details. These attacks work because they are designed to feel urgent and legitimate. Train yourself — and your staff — to pause before clicking anything. If something feels slightly off, it probably is.
  2. Use strong passwords and a password manager. “Lodge123” is not a password. Neither is your Wi-Fi name. A password manager like Bitwarden (free) or 1Password generates and stores unique passwords for every account. You remember one strong passphrase; it remembers the rest.
  3. Turn on multi-factor authentication (MFA). MFA means that even if someone steals your password, they still cannot get in without a second form of verification — usually a code sent to your phone. Enable it on your email first. Then your booking system. Then everything else.
  4. Update software promptly. Software updates are not cosmetic. They close security vulnerabilities that attackers actively exploit. When your phone, laptop, or booking system prompts you to update — do it that day. Not next week.

These four habits, applied consistently across your team, remove the low-hanging fruit that most attackers are looking for. They will not prevent every attack. Nothing will. But they make you a meaningfully harder target — and in a world of rational criminals, harder targets get passed over.

For business owners: the 3Rs

If you run a business with staff, guests, or stored data, the Core 4 is your floor — not your ceiling. For critical infrastructure operators, CISA adds three responsibilities, the 3Rs. They are aimed at much larger organisations than a lodge, but the thinking applies to any business that holds guest data.

  • Reduce your attack surface. Do you still have ex-staff accounts active in your booking system? Are old laptops with guest data sitting in a storeroom? Is your guest Wi-Fi on the same network as your point-of-sale system? Every unnecessary access point is an open door.
  • Replace what is no longer supported. Computers, routers and cameras that no longer receive security updates — Windows 10 machines are the obvious example in many offices — are known weaknesses that attackers look for first. Replace them, and while you are at it, retire shared passwords and put proper controls in their place.
  • Recover with confidence. When something goes wrong — and in a sufficiently long time horizon, something will — your ability to recover quickly is what determines whether an incident becomes a crisis. That means backups tested regularly, a response plan that staff know about, and a notification process that satisfies your legal obligations under POPIA and GDPR.

Privacy law is not optional — and it applies to you

South Africa’s Protection of Personal Information Act (POPIA) has been in full effect since 2021. It requires any business that holds personal data — names, contact details, payment information, ID copies — to protect that data, to report breaches, and to face consequences if they do not.

But here is something many Lowveld businesses do not realise: if you host European guests — and almost every lodge in this region does — the EU’s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) may also apply to you. GDPR follows the data subject, not the location of the business. If a German tourist stays at your lodge and you process their personal information, GDPR obligations attach.

Combined, these two frameworks carry maximum penalties of R10 million and €20 million respectively. More practically: a notifiable breach under either regime triggers a mandatory disclosure process that, handled poorly, becomes a reputational event as damaging as the breach itself.

The solution is not an expensive legal team or an enterprise-grade security department. It is doing the basics correctly, documenting what you hold and why, and having a clear, simple plan for what happens if something goes wrong.

One step, this week

Cybersecurity improvements compound. Each step you take makes the next one easier and the overall posture of your business meaningfully stronger. But the first step has to actually happen — and it has to happen this month, while the urgency is fresh.

Pick one thing from this list and do it before the end of October:

  • Enable MFA on your primary email account today.
  • Audit who has access to your booking or property management system — remove anyone who no longer needs it.
  • Check whether your guest Wi-Fi network is separated from your back-of-house systems.
  • Set up an automated weekly backup for your business-critical files.
  • Run a five-minute phishing awareness conversation with your front-of-house staff.

If you are not sure which one to start with, start with MFA on your email. It is the single change that prevents the largest number of attacks, takes less than ten minutes to set up, and costs nothing.

The criminals who target businesses like yours are not sophisticated hackers in dark rooms. They are organised, patient, and looking for the path of least resistance. Don’t give them one.

October is a prompt, not a deadline. The habits this campaign promotes are not seasonal — they are permanent. But if a global awareness month is what it takes to finally turn on MFA or separate your networks, then make the most of it.
