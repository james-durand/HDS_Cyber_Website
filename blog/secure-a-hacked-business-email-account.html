Changing the password isn’t enough to secure a hacked email account

A password change locks the front door. An attacker who has been inside for a while has often left a window open — here is the full cleanup, step by step.

Someone has got into a business email account. The first instinct is the right one: change the password, fast. That alone stops anyone signing in with the old one.

But it does not end there. A password change locks the front door. An attacker who has been inside for a while has often left a window open — an active session still running, a quiet forwarding rule, a connected app with permission to read the mailbox, a delegate who can open the inbox under their own login, or a recovery method they added to let themselves back in later.

Email deserves this extra care more than almost any other account, because so much hangs off it. Password-reset messages for your accounting software, cloud storage, bank portals, and social media all land in the inbox. Anyone who can still read that inbox may be able to reach those accounts too. That is exactly why the current recovery guidance from Microsoft, Google, and the UK’s National Cyber Security Centre lists several checks beyond changing the password.

Why a new password doesn’t remove every way in

Email services use sessions so you are not typing your password every time you open a message. When you sign in, the service hands your browser or app a token that says “this login is already approved,” and it keeps accepting that token until it expires or someone cancels it.

That is the catch. Someone who signed in before you changed the password may stay connected for a while afterwards, depending on the provider and the type of session.

The providers handle this differently. Google says changing a password signs the user out in most places, but it lists exceptions — some devices and third-party apps can keep working. Microsoft tells administrators to disable the affected business account during the investigation and revoke active sessions as a separate step. And a password change leaves the mailbox settings untouched: any forwarding rule, delegate, or recovery method added while the account was compromised stays in place until someone finds it and removes it.

An old session may still be live

An active session is one of the first things your IT provider should close.

If the attacker signed in through a browser or email app, that session may still hold a valid token — and malware on an employee’s computer can quietly steal a session cookie and pass it to someone else. With a stolen cookie, an attacker can use a login that has already cleared the normal checks. They may not need the new password at all.

The fix is to use the email provider’s tools to revoke the account’s active sessions and tokens. The control goes by different names — “revoke sessions,” “reset sign-in cookies,” “sign out everywhere,” “log out of all devices” — but they do the same job. And if malware might be on the employee’s computer, start the recovery from a different device, and have the affected machine checked before that person signs in again.

Forwarding rules keep your mail flowing out

Once the sessions are closed, look at what was changed inside the mailbox.

An attacker can set up a rule that copies every new email to an outside address. More often they are cleverer than that: a filter that only forwards messages containing words like “invoice,” “payment,” “bank,” or “password reset.” Those selective rules are far harder to spot, because almost everything still arrives normally — the attacker only receives the handful of messages they care about.

Rules can also hide the attacker’s tracks. One might move security warnings, customer replies, or questions about a suspicious payment straight into a folder the employee never opens. Both Microsoft and Google tell users and administrators to check forwarding settings and mailbox rules after a suspected compromise — and in Microsoft 365, to do it through the admin tools, not just the employee’s Outlook settings, because some rules are hidden from the ordinary view.

A connected app can read the mailbox too

Forwarding is one way to keep receiving mail. A connected app is another.

Businesses routinely link email to scheduling tools, customer systems, mobile mail apps, document services, and browser extensions, usually through something called OAuth. When a user approves an OAuth request, the app is granted access to certain parts of the account — without ever needing the password. That is convenient, and it is also exactly what an attacker abuses: they build an app that looks genuine and talk the user into approving it. Microsoft warns that a harmful app-consent grant can hand over email, files, contacts, and forwarding rules in one go.

Some of these connections drop away after a password reset. Others do not, and must be removed on their own. Review every app connected to the account and remove anything the business does not recognise, use, or approve. A trustworthy-looking name and logo prove nothing — your administrator should check the company behind the app and the exact permissions it was given.

Someone else may have mailbox permissions

Business email lets one person open another person’s mailbox. A manager might give a bookkeeper access; a team might share a single “accounts@” or “bookings@” mailbox. This is called delegation, and it matters here because a delegate signs in with their own account — so changing the mailbox owner’s password does nothing to remove them.

Check every person and group with access to the mailbox, confirm why they need it, and remove anything the owner or administrator cannot explain. Pay attention to sending permissions as well: someone with “Send As” access can fire off messages that appear to come straight from the mailbox, even if they cannot read a single email in it.

Check the recovery and sign-in methods

While they had control, the attacker may have added their own phone number, recovery email, authenticator app, passkey, or security key.

Each of these is a way back in. A recovery method lets them reset their way into the account again later; an authentication method might let them approve a sign-in without ever knowing the new password. Microsoft tells administrators to review the account’s registered MFA devices and strip out anything suspicious, and Google says to check recovery details, recent security activity, devices, and sign-in methods. Compare every entry against what the employee actually recognises, and remove the rest.

Securing the account, step by step

Several of these settings are only visible through the business admin tools, so this is a job for your IT provider or email administrator — not something to leave entirely to the employee and their inbox.

  1. Disable the account first. Temporarily suspend it while you investigate, and revoke the active sessions and tokens so the attacker is signed out. The employee loses access briefly, but leaving the account live just gives the attacker more time to read, send, or delete.
  2. Then change the password to one not used anywhere else. If the old password was reused on other accounts, those need new passwords too. Never send the new password to the affected mailbox — use a phone call or an approved password manager. Watch out for older “app passwords,” which Microsoft notes are not automatically cancelled when the main password changes; remove or replace them if they exist.
  3. Review every setting. Forwarding addresses, inbox rules, filters, blocked senders, delegates, sending permissions, signatures, automatic replies, and connected apps — then registered devices, phone numbers, recovery addresses, authenticator apps, passkeys, and security keys. Check POP and IMAP access too, but confirm how the account is actually used first, since some businesses rely on these for approved mail apps.
  4. Work out what happened. Once access is removed, look at the Sent, Deleted, Drafts, Junk, and Archive folders for messages the employee never wrote, replies they never saw, password-reset emails, and any changes to payment instructions. Your administrator should review sign-in logs, audit records, and alerts — starting before the first warning, because the real break-in may have happened earlier. One caution: a login from an unfamiliar place does not prove an intruder. Mobile networks and VPNs distort location; the device, time, IP, app, and surrounding activity together tell you far more than a map pin.
  5. Warn anyone who got a fake message. If the account sent fraudulent email, contact the recipients through a trusted channel — a phone call, not a reply — and tell them which messages were not genuine and what to do. Give special attention to anything about bank details, invoices, payroll, gift cards, or password resets. If money may have gone to the wrong account, call the bank immediately, using a number from its official website or your own records, never one from the email thread.
  6. Secure it before handing it back. Once the unknown access, settings, and permissions are gone, return the account to the employee — but require MFA before they sign in again. Where the platform supports them, passkeys and physical security keys beat codes, because a code can be typed into a fake login page and a passkey cannot. Remember that MFA stops a new sign-in with a stolen password; it does nothing about forwarding rules, delegates, connected apps, or recovery methods that already exist. Those are why the earlier checks still matter.

How to make the next one far less likely

A few settings, put in place once, close most of these doors for good. Ask your IT provider to block automatic forwarding to outside addresses unless your business genuinely needs it, and to limit which third-party apps employees can connect — both Microsoft 365 and Google Workspace have controls for this.

Require MFA on every email account, and especially for administrators and anyone who handles payments or sensitive information, using passkeys or security keys where you can. And make it normal for staff to speak up: an unexpected MFA prompt, an unfamiliar login alert, a mailbox setting that changed on its own, or a message “they” sent but did not write. The sooner that gets reported, the sooner the account can be checked — before more email is sent or quietly deleted.

Frequently asked questions

Does changing an email password sign out every device?

It depends on the provider. Some sign you out of most sessions after a password change; others need a separate step to revoke sessions and tokens. Follow the account-compromise instructions for the service your business uses.

Can someone still receive my email after I change the password?

Yes. A forwarding rule or filter can keep sending them copies. Check all forwarding settings and mailbox rules.

Can an app read my email without knowing my password?

Yes. Through OAuth, an app can be granted access to your account without ever storing the password. Review and remove any connected app the business does not recognise.

Should I delete a compromised email account?

Usually no. It is normally disabled or suspended while it is secured and investigated — deleting it can destroy the records your IT provider needs to work out what happened.

Is MFA enough on its own?

No. MFA helps block a new sign-in with a stolen password, but it does not remove mailbox rules, app permissions, delegates, recovery methods, or active sessions that are already in place. Those have to be checked separately.

The honest bottom line

Changing the password is the right first move — just not the last one. An email account that has been broken into needs a proper clean-out: close the live sessions, clear the forwarding rules, check the connected apps and delegates, remove any recovery methods you don’t recognise, and only then hand it back with MFA switched on.
