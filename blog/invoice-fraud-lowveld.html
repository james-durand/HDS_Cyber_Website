The most expensive email in the lowveld is an invoice

Ransomware gets the headlines. But the losses we see most often around Hoedspruit come from something far quieter — an invoice that arrives from someone you already know, with one line changed.

A packhouse pays its transport contractor at the end of every month. In August the invoice arrives as usual: same layout, same logo, same reference numbers, same friendly note from the same person. The only difference is the account number at the bottom.

Nobody notices. The payment goes out. Three weeks later the real contractor phones to ask where the money is — and by then it has been withdrawn, moved, and is gone.

This is not a technical failure. Nothing was hacked in the way most people picture it. It is a business paying an invoice, which is exactly what a business is supposed to do.

How it actually works

There are two common versions, and it helps to know which one you are dealing with.

1. The lookalike address

The attacker registers a domain that reads correctly at a glance — lowveldtransport.co.za becomes lowveldtranspor.co.za, or .co.za becomes .com. They copy a real invoice they found elsewhere and send it from the new address. On a phone, where the sender often shows only as a display name, there is nothing to see at all.

2. The real mailbox

This one is worse. The attacker has the actual password to someone's email — usually reused from a leaked website, or handed over on a convincing sign-in page weeks earlier. They sit quietly and read. They learn who pays whom, on what cycle, in what tone of voice. Then they reply inside a genuine email thread, from the genuine address, at the moment a payment is due.

No spelling mistakes. No urgency. No foreign prince. Just a normal message from a real colleague or supplier saying the banking details have changed.

The scam does not target your computers. It targets the ordinary trust between people who do business together.

Why the lowveld gets hit harder

A few things about how business works here make this scam land more often than it should:

  • Large, irregular payments are normal. A conveyancing deposit, a season's fertiliser, a lodge refurbishment, a new centre pivot — the amounts are big enough to be worth stealing, and unusual enough that nobody has a reflex for them.
  • Relationships are informal. When you have used the same contractor for eleven years, a request to change bank details from that person does not feel like a red flag. It feels like admin.
  • Approval often sits with one person. On a farm or in a small agency, the same person frequently receives the invoice, approves it and releases the payment. There is no second pair of eyes by design — there is just no second person.
  • Connectivity is patchy. When the line is down and a payment is overdue, everyone becomes more willing to accept “just use these details, we will sort the paperwork later”.

The habit that stops it

Almost every case we have looked at would have been prevented by one rule, and it costs nothing to adopt:

Banking details never change by email. If an invoice, a supplier or a colleague asks you to pay into a new account, you confirm it by voice — on a number you already had before the request arrived.

The last part is what does the work. Attackers put their own phone number in the signature of the email. If you phone the number on the message asking you to change the details, you are phoning the person who sent it. Use the number in your own contacts, on last year's invoice, or on the signed agreement.

Two supporting habits make the rule much harder to get around:

  1. Turn on multi-factor authentication for every mailbox — yours, your bookkeeper's, and anyone who can approve a payment. A stolen password on its own then buys the attacker nothing, which removes the “real mailbox” version of this scam entirely.
  2. Set a rand threshold above which a payment needs two people. It does not have to be sophisticated: a WhatsApp voice note from the second person, kept on file, is enough. Pick a number that hurts to lose — whatever that is for your business — and make it a standing rule rather than a judgement call.
  3. Tell your suppliers you work this way. Say it once, in writing, when you start working together: we will always phone to confirm changed banking details, and we would like you to do the same with us. It makes the confirmation call normal instead of insulting, and it protects them from someone impersonating you.

If you think it has already happened

Speed matters more than anything else here. Money can sometimes be recalled in the first hours, and almost never after a few days.

  1. Phone your bank's fraud line immediately — not the branch, the fraud line — and ask them to attempt a recall. Do this before you investigate anything.
  2. Phone the real supplier on a number you trust, and tell them their name is being used. If their mailbox is the compromised one, every one of their other customers is exposed too.
  3. Change the password on every affected mailbox and turn on multi-factor authentication before you do anything else in it. Then check the mailbox rules — attackers routinely add a hidden rule that auto-deletes or forwards messages so the real person never sees the replies.
  4. Keep everything. Do not delete the fraudulent email. Save it, along with the payment confirmation, for the bank and for SAPS.
  5. Report it. A case number is usually required by your bank and by any insurer, and it is what allows the same account to be flagged when it is used against the next business.

None of this requires new software or a bigger IT budget. It requires a rule that everyone who touches money knows by heart — and the confidence to make a thirty-second phone call before releasing a payment, even when it feels awkward.

That call is the cheapest control in cyber security. It is also, in our experience, the one that has saved lowveld businesses the most money.
