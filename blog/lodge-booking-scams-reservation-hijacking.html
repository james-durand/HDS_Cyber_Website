Booking Scams and Your Lodge

Scammers are using stolen booking details to pose as lodges and ask guests for money. The December holidays are when the fake messages are expected to land.

A guest has booked a December stay with you. Weeks later, a WhatsApp arrives that looks like it is from your lodge. It has their real booking reference and their real dates, and it asks them to pay a deposit into a “new account”. They pay. They arrive in the Lowveld believing the room is settled, and your front desk has no record of the money.

This is called reservation hijacking, and the fake messages are expected to land over the December holidays. You did nothing wrong, but your name is on the message.

What happened

In November 2026, MyBroadband reported that four accommodation platforms used by South African travellers had suffered data breaches this year. The article notes these are only the incidents that have been made public.

  • Booking.com told some South African users their data had been exposed. That could include booking details, names, emails, addresses, phone numbers and anything shared with the property.
  • NightsBridge alerted clients in November. Its review pointed to an AI agent getting into a back-office booking summary list. Names, emails, contact details and booking references were exposed. The company said no payment information or sensitive guest information was involved.
  • LekkeSlaap was named among the platforms breached this year. The article gives no further detail.
  • Hospitality Technology International (HTI) told guests that names, email addresses, phone numbers and booking references were taken through its calendar-sync system, according to researcher Willem Steynberg of Orange Cyberdefense.

Steynberg noted that no card numbers or ID numbers were taken in the two cases he reviewed. That does not make it harmless. A name, a phone number and a real booking reference are enough to build a very convincing fake message.

How the scam works

  1. Booking details are stolen. A platform is breached, or a lodge’s own booking or email account is taken over.
  2. The scammer contacts the guest. The WhatsApp or email uses the lodge’s name and the guest’s real booking reference and dates.
  3. The message asks for money. Usually a deposit into a “new account”, or the balance through a payment link.
  4. It adds pressure. The link expires in five or ten minutes, and the guest is told the booking will be cancelled.
  5. The guest pays the scammer. They arrive believing they have paid the lodge.

The MyBroadband article notes that scammers use AI to write these messages. In our view, that means poor spelling is no longer a reliable warning sign. A fake can read perfectly.

Hi Sarah, this is the lodge. Booking ref HB-48213. Please pay your deposit into our new account today. This link expires in 10 minutes.

That example is invented for illustration, but the pattern is real. Three signs give it away: it asks for payment into a new account, it rushes the guest, and it arrives outside the lodge’s normal way of making contact.

Why this matters for your lodge

As Steynberg points out, the harm falls on people who never dealt with the breached platform directly. Your lodge can be drawn in without any failure on your side.

  • Your name is on the message. The guest believes they paid you, and the hard conversation happens at your reception desk.
  • December is the busy season. More bookings mean more guests who could receive a fake message.
  • Trust is slow to rebuild. A guest who loses money may not separate the scammer from the lodge in a review.
  • The law may apply to you. If your own accounts or systems were involved, POPIA can require you to notify the Information Regulator and the people affected. GDPR may also apply to European guests. We are not lawyers, so please get legal advice on your own situation.

What you can do this week

These five steps cost little, and none of them needs new equipment.

  1. Tell guests how you will contact them and how you take payment. Put it in every booking confirmation. Suggested wording is below.
  2. Never send new bank details by email or WhatsApp alone. If your details ever change, confirm by phone.
  3. Turn on two-step sign-in. Do this for your email and for every booking platform you use.
  4. Give each staff member their own login. Remove logins for people who have left.
  5. Brief your front-of-house staff. They should know what this scam looks like and who to tell when a guest reports one.

Here is wording you can adapt for your booking confirmation:

We only take payment by [your method]. Our bank details do not change, and we will never send you a payment link that expires. If you receive a message that says otherwise, please phone us on [your number] before you pay.

If a guest reports a fake message

Take the report seriously and act the same day. This order puts the guest’s money first.

  1. Thank the guest and ask them not to pay or click. Ask for a screenshot or a forwarded copy of the message.
  2. If they have already paid, ask them to phone their bank straight away. The sooner the bank knows, the better the chance of stopping the payment. They can also report it to SAPS.
  3. Check whether the message came from your own accounts. Look at sent items in your email and at messages on your booking platforms.
  4. If it did, change the passwords and sign out every device. Then turn on two-step sign-in if it was off.
  5. Tell your booking platform. They may already know about a breach and can confirm which guests are affected.
  6. Warn guests with upcoming bookings. A short message telling them how you take payment is enough.
  7. Keep a record. Note the dates, what was sent and what you did. Ask a lawyer whether POPIA requires you to report it.

If your own email account was used, changing the password isn’t enough. Forwarding rules, connected apps and other sessions can let an attacker back in.
