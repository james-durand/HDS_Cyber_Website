When to Replace a Business Computer

Business computers rarely die on a clean, convenient day. They fade. Here is how to tell when it is time to replace one — and when you are about to spend money you did not need to.

Business computers rarely die on a clean, convenient day. They fade. The laptop that used to start in seconds now gives you time to make coffee. The video call to your supplier freezes at the worst moment. The battery that lasted a full day on the road now needs a plug by lunchtime.

So when is it actually time to replace a machine — and when are you about to spend money you did not need to?

For most office computers, three to five years is a sensible planning range. But age is only a prompt to look closer. What really decides the question is whether the computer is still safe to use, still reliable, and still right for the person sitting in front of it.

Start the review at three years

Treat the three-year mark as a diary reminder, not an expiry date.

The big manufacturers plan around this. Dell recommends refreshing business computers every three to four years, and Intel’s refresh programme lets staff on machines older than three years pick a replacement suited to their work. These are planning guides, not rules. A secure, reliable three-year-old laptop does not need to go simply because it has had a birthday.

When a machine turns three, take ten minutes to check five things: the warranty, whether its operating system still gets updates, the battery, the repair history, and what the employee actually needs it to do. If it is likely to be fine for another year, write down the next review date. If problems are already stacking up, add it to the next replacement order.

Reviewing on a schedule does two useful things. It gives you time to budget, and it stops you from having to replace half the office in a single panicked month.

Security support comes first — always

Before you think about speed or battery life, answer one question: can this computer run an operating system that still receives security updates?

If the answer is no, the decision is largely made. Once an operating system reaches the end of support, newly discovered weaknesses may never be patched. Good security software still helps, but it cannot fix holes in the system underneath it. The Australian Cyber Security Centre is blunt on this: unsupported operating systems and networked equipment should be removed or replaced. The UK’s National Cyber Security Centre says the same about obsolete products — keeping them in service raises your risk.

Age helps you plan. Security, condition, and the person using it decide when a computer actually needs to go.

A machine can be in perfect physical health and still need replacing because its hardware simply cannot run a current, supported operating system. In that case, new hardware is safer than nursing an old system along. Some software companies sell short extended-security programmes after normal support ends — useful for buying time to finish a planned replacement, but not a way to keep a computer alive forever.

Ask your IT provider to check the support status of every operating system at least once a year, and to confirm your machines will meet the requirements for the next version before the current one runs out.

Match the computer to the work

A computer that frustrates one person can be perfectly comfortable for another.

Someone who lives in email, documents, and browser-based apps can often stay happy on an older machine for years. Someone wrestling with large spreadsheets, design files, video, or several heavy applications at once will feel an ageing computer much sooner.

The best test is to watch the machine during real work, not a speed test. Does it keep the employee waiting when apps open? Do video calls wobble? Does the person have to close one program before they can open another? Ask them directly how often the computer interrupts their day — a slow start-up once a week is a very different problem from stutters every hour.

Software moves too. Accounting packages, security tools, browsers, and industry apps regularly drop support for older systems and processors. Even if the app still opens, the vendor may no longer test or support it on that setup. Before you renew an important application or move the business to a new platform, check its hardware requirements — that tells you which machines need attention before the change lands, not after.

A slow computer is not always an old computer

Here is the mistake that costs small businesses the most money: assuming “slow” means “finished.”

Plenty of fixable things make a computer crawl — a full storage drive, too little memory, a pile of unnecessary start-up programs, malware, overheating, or a damaged operating system. A failing drive can cause the same symptoms and needs dealing with quickly either way. Ask your IT provider to find the actual cause before you approve a replacement. A clean-up, a repair, a bit more memory, or a new drive may hand you back a machine that feels new.

That said, weigh the fix against the machine’s remaining life. Spending on an upgrade makes little sense if the computer loses operating-system support in a few months, or if other parts are already failing around the one you are fixing.

Add up reliability and repair costs

One repair rarely justifies a new computer. A pattern of them is a different story.

Unexpected shutdowns, failed updates, charging faults, broken ports, storage errors, display glitches, and repeated system repairs can make a machine unreliable even when no single fault looks serious. Review your support requests by device and add up the real cost — the repair bills, the IT time, and the hours the employee could not work properly. A repair that looks cheaper than a replacement is poor value if the next fault is already on its way.

The role matters as much as the machine. A computer failure is far more disruptive for someone who travels, meets customers, or processes payments than for someone doing occasional admin. Keeping one prepared spare — still updated, still running the right software, still checked regularly — can turn a lost day into a lost hour.

Warranty, parts, and batteries

A computer does not need replacing the moment its warranty ends, but from that point the repair bills are yours and the waiting can be longer. Review the warranty before it expires: see whether an extension is offered, what it covers, and how fast the manufacturer responds, then weigh that price against the machine’s age and value.

Check parts availability too. Manufacturers only make replacement parts for so long, and the window differs by model and country — so check the policy for the exact computer, not a general assumption about the brand. Parts matter most when you do not keep spares: a simple repair can still mean several days of downtime if a component has to be ordered in.

Batteries are their own case. They fade with age, and a laptop that once lasted all day may end up tethered to a charger. For a desk-based employee that may not matter at all; for someone on the road it can make the machine unusable. If the computer is otherwise supported, quick, and reliable, a new battery can be money well spent — just confirm the full cost and that parts are available first. One safety note: stop using any laptop whose battery is swollen, unusually hot, or pushing the case out of shape, and have it looked at by someone properly equipped.

Upgrade, replace, or reassign

Some computers respond well to a little investment — more memory, a new drive, a fresh battery. Others are built from parts that are hard or uneconomical to touch.

An upgrade makes sense when a single component is the problem and the machine still has years of security and software support ahead of it. Add up the whole cost — parts, installation, backup, and testing — and compare it with the price and expected life of a replacement. A new machine usually brings a fresh warranty and is easier for your IT provider to manage, but a targeted upgrade can still be the smarter buy when it solves a specific problem cheaply.

And a computer that has outgrown one role may be perfect for another. A machine retired from design or data work can move to someone who mainly uses email and the web — as long as it still gets updates, runs the required software, and behaves itself. Before you hand it over, wipe the previous user’s data and accounts, reconfigure and update it for the new person, check it for faults, and record the new assignment in your inventory. Never reassign a machine that is already unsupported or causing regular trouble — that only delays the same replacement and passes the headache to a colleague.

Keep a simple replacement schedule

The businesses that handle this well are not the ones with the newest kit. They are the ones with a list.

Keep a record of each computer’s purchase date, model, assigned user, warranty expiry, operating system, and next review date. When a machine hits three years, run it through the checks above and give it a planned replacement year. Revisit that date if the person changes roles, the software demands grow, or the operating system’s support timetable shifts.

Spreading purchases across budget periods is far easier than replacing a whole batch in one year, and it gives your IT provider time to order, set up, and test properly. Standardising on a small number of approved models helps too — shared docks and chargers, consistent security settings, and spares that are quick to prepare. Different roles will still need different machines; a general office computer won’t necessarily suit someone working with video, design, or specialist technical software.

Hand over cleanly, and wipe before it leaves

When the new computer arrives, set it up before you retire the old one. Move or back up the employee’s business data, then confirm the new machine has their email, documents, bookmarks, applications, printers, and shared folders — and that multi-factor authentication and any specialist software are working — before they start relying on it.

Once the move is done, take the old machine out of device management, security monitoring, backups, and trusted-device lists, cancel or reassign any licences tied to it, and update your inventory. A short handover period is fine; an old computer left plugged in and unmanaged for months is just more kit to secure.

Then, before that machine is sold, donated, recycled, returned, or thrown out, remove the data properly. Deleting files or removing an account does not guarantee the information is gone. Storage needs to be sanitised using a method suited to the drive, its encryption, and the sensitivity of what was on it — NIST’s Special Publication 800-88 Revision 2 (September 2025) sets out current guidance, and the Australian Cyber Security Centre recommends backing up what you need, unlinking accounts, removing any media, and erasing the device. For particularly sensitive information, use a professional data-destruction service. Keep a disposal record with the asset number, serial number, date, and the method used.

Six questions for every computer review

  1. Can it run an operating system that still gets security updates?
  2. Does it support the software the employee needs?
  3. Does it handle their normal workload without regular delays?
  4. Has it needed repeated repairs or support?
  5. Is it still under a suitable warranty, or are parts easy to get?
  6. Would an upgrade buy enough useful life to justify the cost?

Fail the security or software-support checks, and you plan to replace. For performance and repair questions, compare the cost of fixing it with the likely cost of keeping it another year.

Frequently asked questions

How many years should a business laptop last?

Three to five years is a practical planning range. Replace one sooner if it becomes unreliable, can’t receive security updates, or no longer meets the employee’s needs — and a supported laptop can last longer in a lighter role.

Should every computer be replaced at the same age?

No. Judge each machine on its condition and use. A laptop carried between customer sites every day may need replacing well before a desktop used occasionally for basic admin.

Is a slow computer always ready for replacement?

No. Low storage, too little memory, malware, overheating, or a system fault could be the cause. Have it checked before you decide whether to repair, upgrade, or replace.

Should a computer be replaced when its warranty ends?

Not automatically. The end of the warranty is simply a good moment to review its condition, support status, repair history, importance, and whether you have spares.

Can an old computer be kept as a spare?

Yes — as long as it stays supported, updated, reliable, and managed. Check your spares regularly and confirm they still run the software your team needs.

Can we sell or donate an old computer?

Yes, once all business data is properly removed. Use a suitable wipe process and keep a record of how and when it was disposed of.

The honest bottom line

You do not need the newest computers in the lowveld. You need safe ones, reliable ones, and a simple plan for replacing them before they become a problem. Start the review at three years, put security first, and let condition and the person’s work — not the calendar — make the final call.
