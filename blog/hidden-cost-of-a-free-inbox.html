The Hidden Cost of a Free Inbox

Most small businesses in Hoedspruit start out on a personal Gmail address. It is free, it works, and for the first year nobody thinks twice about it. The problem is that the risk does not stay where you left it.

You have probably been told that @gmail.com looks unprofessional. That is the weakest version of the argument, and it is why most owners ignore it. The real reasons to move are legal, financial and operational — and at least one of them cannot be fixed at any price while you stay on a free account.

POPIA applies to you. There is no small-business exemption.

The Protection of Personal Information Act has no turnover threshold, no headcount threshold and no grace period for sole proprietors. The only carve-out is for processing in the course of a purely personal or household activity — which business correspondence, by definition, is not.

If your mailbox holds customer contact details, guest passport scans, staff records, banking details or learner information, you are a responsible party under POPIA, whether or not anyone has told you. And the Information Regulator has moved from awareness to audit: it has begun issuing compliance notices requiring a written report within 14 business days, covering your security measures, your policies, proof that your Information Officer is registered, staff training in the past year, and your record of breaches.

The problem in one line

A personal Gmail account is not insecure in some abstract sense. It is that it generates no evidence — no admin console, no audit log, nothing to inspect and nothing to show. And the entire enforcement regime is evidence-driven.

The contract Google will not sign

This is the part most owners have never heard, and it settles the argument. POPIA section 21(1) requires that where a third party processes personal information on your behalf, you must ensure — in terms of a written contract — that they maintain the security measures set out in section 19.

Google plainly processes your correspondence. But Google states the following in its own Privacy Help Centre:

“Google doesn’t offer a Data Processing Agreement for the consumer versions of Gmail or Drive.”

“Google does not act as a data processor for the consumer version of these services.”

— Google Privacy Help Centre

That is not a paperwork oversight you can tidy up later. It is structural. There is no version of the free Gmail relationship in which section 21(1) can be satisfied, because Google will not enter into that contract with a consumer account holder. Google Workspace and Microsoft 365 both include exactly this agreement as standard.

What an account takeover actually looks like

Business Email Compromise is the most common serious incident affecting South African small businesses, and it needs no sophisticated malware:

  1. Credentials are harvested — a password reused from an unrelated breach, or a convincing phishing page.
  2. The attacker signs in quietly. With no enforced multi-factor authentication and no admin alerting, nothing flags it.
  3. They read rather than act, learning your clients, your tone and your payment cycle over days or weeks.
  4. A mail rule hides their tracks — replies from one client are auto-archived, so you never see the conversation.
  5. An invoice goes out from your real address, with your real signature, changing the banking details. It passes every technical check, because it genuinely is from you.
  6. The client pays the wrong account. The money is gone within hours.

You now face three problems at once: the financial loss, a POPIA section 22 breach notification you cannot complete because you have no logs to populate it, and a client relationship that may not survive. In 2025 the Information Regulator fined Lancet Laboratories R100 000 purely for failing to notify — not for the breach itself. Insurers, too, increasingly ask whether MFA was enforced and whether audit logs exist.

The enquiries you never knew you lost

Three DNS records — SPF, DKIM and DMARC — tell receiving servers whether a message really came from who it claims. All three live on the domain in your sending address. If that address is @gmail.com, the domain is Google’s, not yours: you cannot create, change or even see those records.

Google, Yahoo and Microsoft now all enforce authentication, and Google’s requirements for every sender — not just bulk senders — include an explicit instruction not to impersonate Gmail From: headers. So your invoicing platform, CRM or booking engine cannot authenticate as you; forwarded mail gets filtered; and website contact forms fail silently, which is the most common and most expensive of the three.

Deliverability failure is invisible from your side. There is no bounce for a message that lands in spam — you simply never hear from the client, and assume they went elsewhere.

Who actually owns the mailbox?

A free Gmail account belongs to the person who created it, under Google’s consumer terms. It is not on your balance sheet and cannot be transferred with the business. When a bookkeeper or manager who used their own Gmail for work resigns, they walk out with the client lists, the pricing and the negotiations — and you cannot revoke it, copy it, or prove what left.

A useful test: if you were hit by a bus tomorrow, could someone else in the business open that mailbox lawfully, find the contract with your largest client, and answer a regulator’s questions about it? If not, the mailbox is a liability regardless of how well it works day to day.

The fix, and what it costs

None of this is difficult or expensive. The order matters, though — doing step two before step one is what causes the “we moved and everything broke” stories.

  1. Register a domain the business owns — around R100 a year, in the business’s name, not an individual’s.
  2. Move to Google Workspace or Microsoft 365. One account per person, never per role.
  3. Publish SPF, DKIM and DMARC. Start DMARC at p=none with a reporting address so you can see who is sending as you before you enforce.
  4. Migrate in parallel, then update every registration: bank, SARS eFiling, CIPC, insurers, booking platforms, suppliers.
  5. Enforce MFA on every account, owners included, and register your Information Officer with the Regulator.

Three questions to ask yourself today

  • Could you, right now, see every sign-in to your mailbox in the past 30 days?
  • If a staff member resigned this afternoon, could you revoke their access and keep their mail?
  • Do you receive DMARC reports showing who is sending email as your business?

Any “no” there is a specific, fixable gap — and none of them take longer than a few weeks to close.
